How to qualify a freelance lead before you write the proposal

Most freelancers qualify with a feeling. The feeling is usually about how nice the person was on the call, which is the least predictive thing about whether the project pays.

Writing a proposal is two to five hours for a small project, and far more for a big one. If a third of your proposals go nowhere, you're spending a day a week on documents nobody reads. Qualification isn't about being picky — it's about spending the proposal hours on the leads where a proposal can actually win.

The six criteria

Score each 1–5. Two low scores are survivable. Four are a project that will cost more than it pays.

1. Fit

Can you do this well and do you want to? Fit isn't "I could figure it out." It's "I have done this before and could show a similar result."

1–24–5
New stack, new industry, or a scope that would be your first attempt at the core deliverable. You can name two previous projects with the same shape, and the client's problem is one you've solved.

2. Budget

Stated or strongly implied, and at or above your minimum. "We'll figure out the budget later" is a 1, not a 3. If they won't name a range before scoping, they usually mean "the cheapest acceptable version."

The question that works: "What range have you set aside for this?" If they can't answer, ask what they paid for the last comparable piece of work. That number is the real budget.

3. Urgency

They have a real deadline or a cost of waiting. A deadline created by their own delay ("we needed this last month") is urgency without planning, which becomes your problem at delivery time.

4. Scope clarity

Deliverable, audience, and success criteria are stated. "A new website" is a 1. "Twelve pages, copy provided, launch before the conference, signup form connected to Mailchimp" is a 5.

5. Decision-maker access

You're talking to the person who signs, or someone with real proxy. A coordinator who "will pass it on" is a 2 — not because they're dishonest, but because your proposal now competes with three other proposals that got a meeting.

6. Low risk

Payment history is clear, no scope traps, timeline is plausible. Red flags here compound: a client who haggled hard on price before scope is agreed will haggle again at every change request.

Interpreting the total

Score (of 30)What to do
24–30Take the call, propose quickly. Speed is a competitive advantage at this tier.
18–23Qualify further. Usually one unknown — budget, or the actual decision-maker. Ask before proposing, not after.
Under 18Decline, or send a paid discovery offer. A small paid step filters out the leads that were never going to buy.

Two composite examples

Both are composites of common patterns, not real clients.

The one that looks good and isn't

A marketing manager wants "a content system" for a product launch in six weeks. Budget: "send a proposal." Scope: "we'll know it when we see it." She's warm, responsive, and has a real deadline.

Fit 4, budget 1, urgency 4, scope 1, decision-maker 2, risk 2 — 14. The warmth and the deadline are doing all the persuading. The missing budget and the missing scope mean you'll write three versions of the proposal and the cheapest one wins.

The one that looks boring and isn't

A small agency needs twelve case-study pages converted from Figma to Webflow, copy is written, launch is tied to a conference in five weeks. They name a range, they've done this before, and the founder is on the call.

Fit 5, budget 4, urgency 5, scope 5, decision-maker 5, risk 4 — 28. Nothing exciting, but the proposal is a formality and the invoice gets paid.

Red flags that override the score

Any one of these should make you pause regardless of the total:

  • "We need it cheap because there's more work later." There usually isn't, and the first price becomes the reference price.
  • Payment terms longer than the project. Net-60 on a three-week project means you're financing them.
  • They've already been through two freelancers. Sometimes bad luck. Twice is a pattern worth asking about directly.
  • Scope described only in negatives. "Not a redesign, just a refresh" tells you what they don't want and nothing about what they'll accept.

The economics, briefly

If your average project is $3,000 and a proposal takes four hours, each proposal costs about $200 of your time at a $90/hour opportunity cost. Ten proposals a month is $2,000 of unpaid work. Cutting three dead-end proposals a month pays for a tool, a course, or a week off.
